# Decisions made while building the V1 slice

Keith gave full permission to make the calls needed to finish. These are the ones that matter, with the reason for each, so anyone can reverse them deliberately.

## Engineering

1. **Godot 4.4.1, GL Compatibility renderer.** It matches the engine of the original web build and is the renderer that runs in browsers. Free.
2. **Rebuilt rather than patched.** The original build shipped only compiled scripts. They were decoded and kept in `docs/recovered_prototype/`. The three systems in them did not line up (the sim engine wrote business intents like "grocery store" while the economy expected "grocery", wages were created from nothing, the ledger only tracked some flows), so the slice was rebuilt as one coherent set of systems that reuse the original names, data and intent.
3. **Money is whole cents in an integer ledger.** Every account sums to exactly zero at all times. The "outside" account is the world beyond the town line and is the only account allowed below zero. This makes "no mystery money" a check that can fail, not a hope.
4. **Systems are static scripts over one data object (`LTWorld`).** No reference cycles, trivial to save, easy to move hot loops to a faster language later as section 14 asks.
5. **Saves are binary chunks per system with a version header.** The first text format lost float precision and a loaded town drifted from the original. Binary keeps the replay exact.
6. **Determinism.** One seeded random generator, arrays in id order, all player actions logged with the day. The acceptance run replays and compares.
7. **Hourly routines are visual only.** The daily tick is the truth. Watch mode derives where a Sim is at each hour from their real job, school, home and the shops they use. This follows section 9 and the tier rule in section 14.
8. **Draw call budget.** Each lot is merged into one mesh with vertex colors (plus one for lit windows), roads are one mesh, trees are one mesh. Sims near the camera are capped at 45.

## Simulation numbers (all in one place in each system, easy to tune)

9. **Prices.** One person-day of groceries costs $8 at the outside supermarket. Wholesale from Valley Wholesale costs 70% of that plus a delivery fee and a 2 day delay (4 in snow or storms). Local farm produce sells to the grocery at about half the retail price. The surplus goes to an outside co-op at a low price.
10. **Business start up costs** are sized to what small town Sims could fund with savings, family money and a bank loan: grocery $32,000, farm $24,000, general store $18,000, construction company $16,000, cafe $15,000. A town of 100 can barely carry a grocery, which is realistic, and is exactly where the mayor's incentives matter.
11. **Founding needs a planning period.** A Sim plans for about three weeks (two if the city published the need) before committing, and writes "started planning" to their history. This stops herd starts on day one and gives the player time to see it coming.
12. **Outside jobs and wages** pay $110 to $170 a day by job and skill. Commuting costs $7 a day in fuel. Local jobs pay a little less but save the drive, so Sims switch when a local job opens.
13. **The city budget** gets property tax (1.5% a year), sales tax (2%), license fees, lot leases, business tax (6% of profit) and state aid ($22 per resident per month, as small US towns receive). The state pays most school costs; the city pays a local share per student. The county sheriff and fire service are contracts. Starting cash is $150,000.
14. **Bank lending tightness** rises with the bank's loss ratio on its loan book and with bankruptcies per 100 people, and relaxes slowly. This is the required feedback loop: a bankruptcy wave raises the credit bar and the rates for the next founders.
15. **Bankruptcy rules (simplified, flagged for review).** Liquidation keeps $7,000 of cash exempt, the trustee takes a 10% fee and pays unsecured creditors pro rata, secured debt in arrears is surrendered, sole proprietor businesses are liquidated. Repayment plans run 36 months under a $3,200 monthly income and 60 above, at 70% of spare income, and are dismissed after three missed payments. Tax, child support and fraud debts are never discharged. The filing blocks new bank credit for two years and weighs on the score for ten.
16. **Estates follow the order in section 7.6.** Joint ownership passes the house to the spouse outside the estate. The mortgage travels with the house. Then funeral and administration, taxes, secured, unsecured. Joint and co-signed debts pass to the survivor. Heirs never inherit debt. Paying a dead relative's bills, including a funeral the estate cannot cover, is a scored choice, never automatic. South Dakota rules: not a community property state. The homestead exemption is simplified to "the house goes to the surviving spouse or heir who takes it".
17. **A church food pantry exists** (funded from outside donations) so a broke household eats something. Without it, early builds starved Sims to death in a few months, which is neither realistic nor the story the design wants. Going to the pantry is logged.
18. **Petitions build slowly.** Nobody organizes in the first two weeks, at most one new petition starts a week, and signatures grow by roughly one or two a day. The first build had three petitions on the mayor's desk by January 3.
19. **Birth and death rates** were tuned against US rates for a town of about 100 (roughly one birth and one death a year, with variation by seed).
20. **Theft is a scoring hook only**, as the slice scope says. Desperate, dishonest Sims may shoplift or steal from a neighbor; police funding sets the chance of being caught; a record follows them into job applications.
21. **Outside contractors** keep the four names from the original prototype, each with its own honesty, greed, skill and crew. Riverbend Contractors low bids and plans change orders; its roads wear out fast. A dishonest bidder may offer the city clerk a kickback, and the clerk's honesty decides.
22. **Town name** "Prairie Hollow" and business names are placeholders.

## Building the full design (second pass)

28. **Nothing in the design is left out.** Keith asked for everything, so the systems the slice deferred are built: the full law list, every business type on the growth path, crime and courts, health and addiction, divorce and child support, the county region, districts, bonds, the bus, the newspaper, elections and sound.
29. **Elections are an optional mode, off by default.** Locked decision 3 says the player stays mayor indefinitely in V1, and section 17 asks for elections as an optional mode. Both hold: turn it on from the Mayor desk. With it on, a challenger runs on the town's real grievances, adults vote on trust and their own lives, and a long run of very low approval can force a recall.
30. **Multiplayer is not built.** The design never designs it, and its platform target is "single player, offline". Building it would contradict a locked decision, so it stays out until Keith designs it.
31. **Direct family control stays watch only**, which is the answer section 20 gives for V1. Following a household is fully supported through the watch list.
32. **Service spending goes through one spend book.** Doctor visits, prescriptions, repairs, child care, tuition, funerals, lawyers, realtor commissions, freight, storage, lodging and bars are paid through `LTServices.spend`, which picks a local Sim run business when one is open and fair, or sends the money out of town. Founders read the book to see what the town lacks.
33. **Large start ups can take outside investors** (factories, banks, hotels and anything over $40,000), who put in money for a share of the owner's draws. Without that, no Sim could ever raise $60,000 to $90,000 alone.
34. **The local bank needs a city charter and at least 120 people.** Its loans replace the outside bank's for new borrowers, its interest income stays in town, and its losses fall on a local owner.
35. **Crime rules.** Theft, burglary, fraud, tax evasion, unlicensed businesses, assault in unsafe places, drug possession and contempt for unpaid child support. Police funding sets detection. Trials come two to five weeks after a charge. A lawyer lowers the chance of conviction and the sentence. First minor offenses get probation; felonies and repeat offenses get jail, which costs a job after ten days. Restitution and fraud debts survive bankruptcy.
36. **Health rules.** Pregnancy lasts about nine months with checkups and six weeks of leave, and an outside employer may not hold the job. Risky jobs (construction, farm, factory, trucking) carry injury risk that skill lowers; a bad injury can leave a lasting disability that rules out heavy work. Long hardship can bring depression, eased by counseling. Drinking, gambling and drugs are scored coping choices with real costs; treatment is a choice pushed by family, discipline, age and city health support, and relapse is possible.
37. **Divorce.** Marriages wear down under money stress, addiction, jail and unhappiness, and build up with kindness. Child support is about 17% of income for one child, more for more, until the youngest is 18, and climbs the same collection ladder as any debt, with contempt charges after three missed months.
38. **The region** runs a slow business cycle (steady, growth, slowdown, recession, recovery) that sets outside hiring, layoffs, export prices and travelers. Six named county employers rise and fall; a failure lays off every town Sim who worked there.
39. **Annexed land** arrives unzoned with a gravel street that must be paved through bids. The exit to the highway and every lot's street are stored, so the graph can grow without breaking paths.
40. **Sound is synthesized in code** at a low sample rate (wind, rain, birds, chimes), so there are no audio files and it runs in the browser. Sound can be turned off from the Menu button in the top bar.
41. **Performance.** Per day caches for market estimates, job seekers, debt stress, safety and open sites keep about 930 Sims near 150 ms per day. What remains is plain per Sim work, the hot loop section 14 expects to move to a faster language later.

## Founding a town and real time (third pass, Keith's direction)

42. **Two starts, two kinds of mayor.** "Found a town" (alone, with lottery money) and "Prairie Hollow" (about 100 people). Each can be permanent mayor or elected mayor, chosen at the start instead of toggled mid game.
43. **The player is a permanent resident with no needs,** living in a camper until the mayor's house (a $300,000 project through bids) is built, then in the house. Their only goal is running the town. They are not a simulated Sim, so they never take a job, a loan or a vote.
44. **Before incorporation the lottery money is the town fund.** The county taxes and serves an unincorporated settlement, so the fund only pays the town's own bills (land, roads, the mayor's house, incentives) and earns from lot sales. At 25 residents the town incorporates: city taxes, services, a clerk, and in elected mode a first election 90 days later.
45. **Settlers build their own homes.** A family buys a zoned residential lot from the town fund at land value, hires a builder through private bids, and the bank's construction loan becomes their mortgage when they move in. Their pull is the county economy, cheap land, shops, school, clinic, safety, jobs, and paved roads, minus taxes.
46. **Time stops when the game is closed.** No offline progress. Live speed is one game second per real second. "Today" fast forwards a few days per frame to the real date (never backward) and sets the clock to the real time of day. The real calendar has no leap days: February 29 reads as February 28.
47. **Milestones** add goals without forcing outcomes: they only notice what the Sims and the player achieved.
48. **Bug fixed along the way:** with nobody living in town, approval read as zero and triggered a recall election against no one. Elections now wait for incorporation.

## Open land and the camera (fourth pass)

49. **A founded town starts as open land.** One county road crosses the map edge to edge and nothing else is laid out. Roads snap to a survey grid of 32 by 24 blocks, straight in one direction per stroke, which keeps travel times, lot access and bidding simple while leaving the layout to the player. Each drawn road becomes a project to bid on (about $22,000 a block) and is staked but unusable until a contractor builds it. Lots can be plotted beside any road, built or planned, in any zone; settlers and founders only use lots a built road connects to the county road. Empty city lots can be returned to prairie. Prairie Hollow keeps its ready-made grid.
50. **The camera** now moves toward goal positions and glides there each frame, reads the mouse buttons actually held instead of remembering presses (a release over a panel used to leave it spinning or panning), treats two fingers as one pinch, twist and pan gesture, stays over the map, and never follows a Sim out onto the highway.

## Contracts, lot details and street level sound (fifth pass)

51. **The mayor writes every public contract before it is signed.** Tap a planned road (or open any city project) to set its terms: surface (gravel, asphalt, concrete), width (narrow lane, two lane street, wide avenue), sidewalks, streetlights, materials grade for buildings, a deadline with an optional late penalty, and the budget ceiling. Terms set the fair price and schedule contractors bid against, the quality of what is built, how fast it wears, and how fast traffic moves on it. Lit streets raise the town's safety and sidewalks draw settler families. A deadline shorter than a crew's honest schedule is met with rush pricing. Changing terms after bids are in sets those bids aside and makes the contract a draft again, because every price was for the old terms; it goes back out only when the mayor presses Go live. Once a contract is signed only the road's name can change.
51a. **Contracts start as drafts.** Drawing a road, drafting a city project, or approving a petition for a public build makes a private draft. Contractors cannot see it until the mayor presses **Go live**, so the terms can be worked out with no clock running. Nothing the city pays for ever goes out to bid on its own: drawn roads, drafted projects, petition builds (including apartments) and re-edited contracts all wait for Go live. Settlers' houses and developers' apartments are the owners' own jobs: they pick their builders, and they are listed apart as private builds. A drawn road opens its contract right away; a draft road is chalked out pale on the map and labeled "(draft)", and turns to staked dirt once it is live.
52. **Lots plotted together can be edited together.** Tap a city lot to set its zone, asking price (50% to 200% of land value), whether the city holds it back from sale and lease, and a name. Families look at the cheapest lots first, and above full value many keep looking. Commercial leases follow the asking price.
53. **The town is only heard at street level.** Wind, rain, birds, night crickets, passing cars, hammering at building sites and voices fade in at the same zoom where Sims appear, and are silent on the planning map. Event chimes belong to the mayor's desk, so they always play. Sims are not drawn above street level either, which also saves work when zoomed out.

## Realistic odds and the player's profile (sixth pass)

54. **The world is real, and the Sims are as real as we can make them.** Weather, storms, blizzards, tornadoes, fires, workplace accidents, insurance, the county economy and the starting population are matched to real US and South Dakota figures and the test fails if they drift. Sims still decide everything from their own traits, needs and relationships, but their decision rules are tuned so the results land near how real small town people live: about as many marriages, children and divorces, owners who ride out a bad season, founders who learn from a neighbor's failure, and bankruptcy only over debts worth filing for. Those rates are listed beside the human figures for reference. `tests/realism.gd` runs the weather alone for 200 years, the county economy for 100 years and a 1,000 person town for three years; world rates must fall in their real ranges, and the Sims' rates are listed beside the human figures for reference. The report is `reports/realism_summary.txt`.
55. **Weather follows the real climate of eastern South Dakota by month** (NOAA normals for Sioux Falls): about 40 thunderstorm days, 34 snow days and 100 wet days a year, almost no winter thunder.
56. **Disasters are rare and fall on the weak.** Severe thunderstorms reach town about 1.5 times a year (May to August), blizzards about 1.5 times (November to April), and a tornado about once in 400 years. Ordinary thunderstorms and snow only damage buildings that are run down (condition under 45) or were built cheaply (quality under 0.4). Severe storms dent about 1 sound roof in 10 with hail and hit weak buildings hard. A stricter building code lowers all of it. Blizzards close roads for the day.
57. **Fires follow NFPA rates:** about 1 home in 400 a year, more in winter and in run down buildings. Most are small; about 1 in 20 destroys the house; about 1 in 30 injures someone and 1 in 125 kills someone. Fires now happen before the town incorporates too.
58. **Homeowners insurance:** owners pay about 0.6% of the building's value a year and insurers pay repairs above a $1,500 deductible. A household that cannot pay lets the policy lapse and pays any damage in full.
59. **The starting town** has the age mix and household types of a small midwestern town in the census (about 13% under 10, a quarter over 60).
60. **Rules of the world fixed along the way:** a units bug made founders skip the business license far too often; a first unlicensed offense now means a fine and buying the license, not closure; a bankruptcy filing costs about $1,650 in lawyer and court fees.
61. **The player has a profile:** name, pronouns, age (which grows with the calendar), hometown and a color that trims the camper and the mayor's house. All of it, and the town's name, can be changed any time from the Mayor tab.

62. **Bigger buttons.** The time controls and the build tools are at least 48 pixels tall with larger text, the size touch guidelines ask for. To make room, Save, Load, New town, Your profile and Sound moved into one Menu button at the right end of the top bar.

63. **Small town crime is rare.** In a town of a few hundred everyone knows everyone: a thief is somebody's cousin and a stranger's car gets noticed. Crime chances scale with how anonymous the town is (near zero under a few hundred people, full at about 3,000), even desperate Sims think twice, and neighbors raise the chance a crime is solved.
64. **More choices for Sims,** each from their own traits, needs and relationships and written in their history with the reason: extra work when money is tight (more pay, less rest and family time), night classes at the county college to raise a skill (savings or a small student loan), volunteering (the volunteer fire department, which makes fires smaller, the food pantry, coaching, meals for seniors), helping family or friends through a bad month (a gift or an interest free loan), and caring for an aging parent (daily visits, or moving them in). The Sim panel shows what each Sim is doing.
65. **Autosave and undo.** The game saves itself every few game days, after each change the mayor makes, and whenever the app is closed or sent to the background, and it opens where you left off. Undo takes back the mayor's last changes until the next day begins.
66. **Map overlays and next steps.** The tool bar can color every lot by value, condition, happiness, safety or what the city has for sale. A small card under the top bar lists the next things to do in a new town, then the milestones ahead.
67. **Snow stays.** Snowfall builds up on the ground and on roofs and melts slowly in deep winter and quickly in spring, so the prairie stays white through most of a Dakota winter.

68. **How Sims move on screen.** Trips take real time by distance (walking about 1.4 m/s, driving about 40 km/h in town), so in Live a walk to work takes a few real minutes and Sims at home or work potter about every few minutes instead of standing frozen. In Watch each trip lasts at least about a second of real time so it can be followed. At day by day speeds (Normal, Fast, Turbo) everyone is shown still, where they are at half past noon, and moves to their new spot only when the next day comes. Pausing freezes everyone exactly where they are. A Sim only snaps to a new spot while out of sight.

69. **Starting a business costs what it really costs.** Each kind of business has a building size, equipment and opening stock (bought used where people really do), plus three months of cash and the license; the town's size scales the building and stock, so a 100 person town gets a corner market, not a supermarket. A new building is a real construction job: the founder hires a builder through bids (a 20 year commercial mortgage pays for it) and the business opens the day it is finished. A founder can instead lease an empty building a closed business left behind. Contractors, mechanics, home daycares, cottage bakeries, lawyers, realtors and owner operator truckers can start from home, as most do in small towns: a contractor needs about $49,000 for a used truck, trailer and tools. Banks lend with about 10% down (5% with a city guarantee). The Sim's history and the business panel show every dollar and where it came from.
70. **Every house is what its family chose:** bedrooms, bathrooms, one or two stories, a garage with a stall per car, and a basement. A settler family plans the house around who they are (a room for the couple and about one for every one or two children, a second bath as the family grows) and their personality (ambitious families reach for more, careful ones build what they need), then trims it to what the bank will lend (about four times their income). It costs about $175 a square foot, $18,000 a garage stall and $35,000 for a basement. The house on the map is drawn from it, and the lot panel lists it.
71. **Every kind of business has its own building:** the grocery, cafe, general store, bakery, pharmacy, repair garage, law office, realty office and academy each have their own look, joining the farm, mill, factory, warehouse, trucking yard, motel, bank, clinic, tavern, child care center and funeral home. A business run from home shows a yard sign, and a contractor's or trucker's work truck in the drive.

72. **What Sims do with their time, on screen.** Sims have faces, so you can see which way they look. Like real people they spend most of their time indoors, out of sight: at work they step out front only for a mid morning break, lunch and a mid afternoon break, and children only for recess. At home, each stretch of free time is a choice you can read: sitting in a lawn chair on the front lawn facing the street (older Sims more often), kneeling at the flower bed (not in winter), walking down the sidewalk and back, or for children, playing in the yard; nobody goes out in the rain or a storm. Every lived in house has two lawn chairs and a flower bed.

73. **Panels made for thumbs.** The sections are two rows of big buttons instead of small tabs. Text, buttons, dropdowns and checkboxes are larger, scroll bars are about a finger wide, and a drag that starts on text or a button scrolls the panel instead of getting stuck. While someone is scrolling a panel it is not rebuilt under them (it catches up a couple of seconds after they stop), and a rebuilt panel keeps its scroll position.

## Scope calls

23. **The outside bank** (Prairie State Bank) serves the town until the city charters a local one (item 34).
24. **Schools and clinics exist both ways:** city services built through bids and staffed by city hires, and private Sim run clinics and academies that compete with them.
25. **The independent review gate** in section 18 needs a person who did not write the code. Everything else in that section is automated in `tests/acceptance.gd` and `tests/scenarios.gd`.
